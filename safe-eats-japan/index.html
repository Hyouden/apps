What she can eat

Dairy is the one hard rule. Traditional Japanese food barely uses it — the danger is butter, cream and cheese in grills, ramen, Western-style and café food. Wheat: skip noodles, bread and batter, but soy sauce and shared fryers are fine. Soy: a little is fine.

Set up before you fly

Put it on your home screen & save offline

  • iPhone: open in Safari → Share button → Add to Home Screen.
  • Android: Chrome menu ⋮ → Add to Home screen / Install app.

The card, phrases and guide already work offline after the first open. This also saves the food photos.

Pack & prep
  • Let the hotel call ahead. Front desks will phone a restaurant for you. Phrases → Allergy has a card to show them: このお店に、乳製品抜きの料理に対応できるか電話で聞いていただけますか？
  • Chains have allergen charts (アレルギー表) — ask for one. Milk (乳) is always listed, so it's the fastest yes/no.
  • Google Translate camera on a menu, then show the card for anything unclear.
  • Email hotels & any ryokan dinners ahead — Kaiseki is mostly dairy-free, but modern courses slip in butter or cream; kitchens adapt well with notice. This note is more detailed than the card:
  • Install HappyCow (vegan = guaranteed dairy-free) and Find Me Gluten Free, and star the Places in this app in Google Maps.
  • Travel days: most ekiben (station bento) are fine now that soy sauce is OK — skip ones with hamburg steak, gratin or croquettes. Onigiri, eggs and sweet potato from a konbini are easy backups.
  • Hotel breakfast buffets: the Japanese side (rice, grilled fish, miso soup, pickles, natto) is the safe side. Skip butter, yogurt, bread, pastries and the omelette station.
Drinks
✅ Usually fine
Water, green tea, barley tea, hōjicha, sake, shochu, highballs, umeshu, wine, black coffee, oat-milk lattes
❌ Watch out
Café & matcha lattes, milk tea, canned coffee (most have milk), hot chocolate, cream liqueurs, beer (barley)
